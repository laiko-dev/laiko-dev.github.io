# For Subbies: Sending a SWMS Template the Principal Contractor Will Accept First Time

If you subcontract on commercial jobs or larger residential builds, you have probably had a SWMS template bounced back by a site manager. It costs a day, sometimes a start date, and it is rarely about safety itself. Usually something in the document doesn't line up with the project.

Under the model WHS laws, a principal contractor on a construction project has to take reasonable steps to obtain a SWMS before high risk construction work starts. So the site team reads what you send, and they read a lot of them. This guide covers how to send one that gets through.

## Why statements get sent back

The usual reasons are predictable:

- **Wrong project details.** An old address, the wrong project name or a different builder's logo carried over from the last job.
- **Too generic.** A statement that could apply to any job by your trade, rather than this scope on this site.
- **High risk categories missing.** The work involves a fall risk over 2 metres or powered mobile plant, but the statement doesn't name it.
- **Ignores site rules.** The project has its own permit system, exclusion zones or hours for plant, and the statement doesn't reflect them.
- **Controls lean on PPE.** Harnesses and gloves everywhere, very little elimination, isolation or engineering.
- **No sign-on sheet** or no names for the workers doing the job.

## Read the site rules first

Before drafting anything, ask the principal contractor for whatever they issue to trades: the site safety plan or a summary, induction material and any permit requirements, such as for hot works, penetrations or crane lifts. Note where the site sets rules that affect your method, like delivery windows, set-down areas or shared scaffold access.

Those details belong in your statement. A site manager who sees their own rules reflected back is far more likely to accept it on the first pass.

## Before you hit send

Run through this:

- Project name and address match the principal contractor's documents
- Your business details are correct
- Tasks follow your actual scope, in the order you will do them
- Every applicable high risk category is listed
- Site rules and permits are reflected in the controls
- Emergency arrangements line up with the site's emergency plan
- Worker names or a sign-on sheet are included
- The date is current

## Don't forget the interfaces

Subcontractors work around other trades, and that is where a lot of risk sits. Think about:

- Working above or below another crew
- Sharing scaffold or hoists
- Your plant moving through areas where others are working
- Dust, noise or fumes from your work affecting other trades

Site managers notice when a subbie has thought about the people around them, not just their own crew.

## When the scope changes

If a variation changes what you are doing, how you are doing it or where, the statement needs a review before the changed work starts. Re-describe the new scope, check the updated draft against the site, and resend it. It is quicker than explaining later why the paperwork didn't match.
